# Measure distance to a frontier-safety standard

Research report, September 2026. Written for PRD 15 and PRD 16 from five parallel literature and policy reviews; sources are cited inline. Country placements in the anchor tables are illustrations, not a scoring run.

The app should add a separate dimension called Frontier Risk Governance and leave the existing maturity scores alone. The new dimension scores each country against a published standard for managing catastrophic risk from frontier AI, drawn from the x-risk governance literature. It runs on applicability tracks, so the roughly 190 countries with no frontier developer are not scored as failures, and one weak element caps the score so that activity elsewhere cannot hide the gap. The research supports the maintainer's concern. Every maintained country index (GIRAI, Oxford Insights, the OECD.AI Index, CAIDP) measures readiness, rights or capacity. At most they carry one binary item relevant to frontier risk, usually membership of the AI Safety Institute network. All of the frontier-risk scorecards (FLI, SaferAI, METR, Midas) rate labs, not states. On what good national governance means, the AI safety community broadly agrees on a stack of levers:

- a compute or capability threshold that defines which models are frontier models
- mandatory safety and security frameworks
- government or independent evaluation before deployment
- incident reporting on fixed deadlines
- whistleblower protection
- security for model weights
- compute governance
- a technical state evaluation body
- emergency authority to act in a crisis
- international coordination

Several of these levers have no enacted example anywhere in September 2026. That is informative: a 5 on this dimension should be rare. The main risks are political more than technical. A score framed around x-risk will be read as effective-altruism advocacy unless it states its value standard, credits non-Western instruments by what they do, and separates "no evidence" from "no activity". Research on LLM coding also shows that anchors have to describe checkable artefacts, not evaluative labels.

## No maintained index scores national governance of frontier risk

Country-level AI indices and frontier-risk scorecards measure different things. The frontier-risk scorecards all rate companies. The **FLI AI Safety Index (Summer 2026)** grades 9 companies on 37 indicators in six domains, including an "Existential Safety" domain in which no company scored above C-. Its top overall grade was Anthropic's C+ (2.66) ([FLI](https://futureoflife.org/ai-safety-index-summer-2026/)). **SaferAI's tracker** rates 12 companies' risk management from their published frontier safety frameworks. Scores run from 8% to 35% against a best-practice ceiling of roughly 54 to 59%; the tracker and the paper disagree on the exact ceiling ([SaferAI](https://tracker.safer-ai.org/); [arXiv 2512.01166](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.01166)). METR compares the common elements of lab safety policies without scoring them ([METR](https://metr.org/common-elements)). AI Lab Watch stopped maintenance in September 2025 ([AI Lab Watch](https://ailabwatch.org/)). FLI's only government-facing scorecard, from 2023, rated policy proposals, not countries ([FLI](https://futureoflife.org/document/fli-governance-scorecard-and-safety-standards-policy/)).

The country indices measure other constructs, and their headline results diverge accordingly. The **Global Index on Responsible AI 2026** covers 135 countries with 38 indicators. It puts the United States 27th (53.34) and China 42nd (43.58), yet China's Trust & Safety score (64.16) is above the US score (59.21) ([GIRAI 2026](https://www.global-index.ai/downloads/Global-Index-on-Responsible-AI-2026.pdf)). The **Oxford Insights Government AI Readiness Index** ranks the US first (87.20) and China eighth. Its Resilience pillar "measures how countries are engaging with AI safety and security", mainly through AISI-network membership ([Oxford Insights](https://oxfordinsights.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/LATEST-GAIRI-Report-2025.pdf)). The **OECD.AI Index** (February 2026) covers OECD members only. Its frontier-relevant content is a single indicator for membership of the International Network of AI Safety Institutes, plus Epoch AI counts of large models and GPU clusters. It lists "Mandatory Security and Safety Testing Policies for AI Development" and "Availability of Incident Response Manuals" only as possible future extensions, because the data do not exist ([OECD.AI Index paper](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2026/02/oecd-ai-observatory-index_8f5fa0f2/32c01014-en.pdf)). The International AI Safety Report scores no countries ([IASR 2026](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026)).

The nearest precedents are two academic studies from August 2026, and neither is maintained. **"Mapping General-Purpose AI Governance in Twenty AI Middle-Power Jurisdictions"** codes four links in an accountability chain at the model layer: systemic-risk assessment, evaluation and verification, prohibitions with monitoring, and serious-incident reporting. It records confirmed absence as data. It finds that jurisdictions "converge on form, but diverge on force": only about **20% of provisions are binding**, and **outside the EU no jurisdiction imposes binding evaluation duties on model developers** ([arXiv 2608.19278](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19278)). **"An Evaluation Framework for National AI Regulation"** uses ordered anchors tied to specific provisions, with a documented search and cutoff protocol, close to the app's own rubric method. Its authors say it evaluates "what a portfolio provides on paper" and not enforcement or outcomes ([arXiv 2608.15417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15417)). A country-level frontier-risk dimension would therefore fill a real gap. It would also have no validated predecessor to calibrate against.

## Existing indices reward paper, volume and compensation

The critiques of the existing indices fall into five groups. The first is **paper versus practice**. SaferAI concedes that its score measures "the maturity and completeness of a company's documented risk-management process… not a direct measurement of model behavior" ([SaferAI](https://tracker.safer-ai.org/)). The Global Health Security Index is the cautionary case from a neighbouring field. It scored 195 countries on preparedness capacity, and one analysis found its scores associated with *worse* COVID-19 outcomes, with correlations with mortality reaching r = -0.71 by the end of 2021 ([Goldschmidt, PMC10910940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10910940/)). A frontier-risk score built only from "has a law, has an institute, signed a declaration" items could fail the same way.

The second is **form over force, and the EU transposition effect**. GIRAI finds 78% of Global South frameworks non-binding against 42% in the Global North. Nine of the ten European countries that newly gained a Safety & Security framework got it through the EU AI Act ([GIRAI 2026](https://www.global-index.ai/downloads/Global-Index-on-Responsible-AI-2026.pdf)). A dimension that counts instruments lifts all 27 member states at once for one EU-level act.

The third is **compensatory aggregation**, which bears directly on the app's arithmetic mean. The OECD/JRC Handbook on composite indicators notes that linear aggregation implies full compensability: weakness on one indicator is offset by strength on another. It recommends geometric or non-compensatory methods when components are not substitutes ([OECD/JRC Handbook](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2008/08/handbook-on-constructing-composite-indicators-methodology-and-user-guide_g1gh9301/9789264043466-en.pdf)). The OECD.AI Index's own top performers (US, UK, Switzerland) rank there partly on R&D strength ([OECD.AI Index paper](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2026/02/oecd-ai-observatory-index_8f5fa0f2/32c01014-en.pdf)). Under the app's current rule, a country with three documented instruments and nothing operating still averages close to 4.

The fourth is **Western and visibility bias**. Readiness indices "reward formal strategies and patents while overlooking… adaptive governance capacities" ([Levy Yeyati, IDB](https://publications.iadb.org/publications/english/document/Are-We-Ready-for-AI-From-Measurement-to-Policy-Governance.pdf)). Oxford Insights says China's rank "likely understates" its capacity because of limited transparency ([Oxford Insights](https://oxfordinsights.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/LATEST-GAIRI-Report-2025.pdf)).

The fifth is **fragile binary proxies**. AISI-network membership is the one frontier item most indices use, and it has drifted. The US institute became the Center for AI Standards and Innovation and the UK's became the AI *Security* Institute. In December 2025 the network renamed itself the International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science ([Wikipedia: AISI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_safety_institute); [UK AISI](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/international-ai-network-consensus-and-open-questions)). IAPS notes that first-wave AISIs have **no regulatory powers** ([IAPS](https://www.iaps.ai/research/understanding-aisis)). Membership says nothing about access to models, budget or authority.

The reference governance indices outside AI show how to answer all five. Freedom House states its normative basis (the Universal Declaration of Human Rights) and scores rights "enjoyed" rather than guaranteed on paper. It changes a score only when a real-world development warrants it ([Freedom House](https://freedomhouse.org/reports/freedom-world/freedom-world-research-methodology)). The Climate Action Tracker rates promises separately from implemented policy, against an external benchmark (1.5°C pathways), and it does not count instruments ([CAT](https://climateactiontracker.org/countries/rating-system/)). The Environmental Performance Index normalises each indicator as distance to a target, set where possible by scientific consensus such as WHO guidelines ([EPI 2024](https://epi.yale.edu/downloads/2024epitechnicalappendix20241207.pdf)). This distance-to-target pattern is the most direct fix for "5 = implemented, not good". It keeps the implemented-versus-not question but asks whether the implemented thing meets a named standard.

## The x-risk literature converges on a lifecycle stack of ten levers

EA-aligned and AI safety organisations disagree on strategy, but they largely agree on which national measures matter. The 2023 GovAI-led paper "Frontier AI Regulation" set out the core: standard-setting, registration and reporting for regulatory visibility, and compliance mechanisms up to licensing, with pre-deployment risk assessment and external scrutiny ([arXiv 2307.03718](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03718)). GovAI's compute-governance work argues that compute is "detectable, excludable, and quantifiable" and comes from a concentrated supply chain. That makes it the most governable input, supporting visibility, allocation and enforcement ([GovAI](https://www.governance.ai/research-paper/computing-power-and-the-governance-of-artificial-intelligence)).

The most current agenda is **IAPS's "Priorities for Frontier AI Policy"** of 11 September 2026. It moves the frontier of the standard forward on several fronts ([IAPS](https://www.iaps.ai/research/priorities-for-frontier-ai-policy)):

- mandatory government access and evaluation before consequential *internal* deployment, not only public release, with a focus on AI that automates AI R&D
- regular risk reports and harmonised reporting of incidents and near-misses
- funded independent evaluators and evaluator standards
- a graduated standard for model-weight security on RAND's SL1–SL5 scale
- export controls with chip-location verification and authority over foreign remote access to compute
- safety cases for high-stakes development
- a National AI Reserve Corps and a Rapid Emergency Assessment Council for crises

The International AI Safety Report 2026 supplies the empirical case. The number of companies publishing frontier safety frameworks "more than doubled" in 2025, but participation stays voluntary. An "evaluation gap" means pre-deployment tests do not reliably predict real-world behaviour. And "lack of incident reporting and monitoring makes it difficult to assess how well current practices reduce risks" ([IASR 2026 summary](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-extended-summary-policymakers)).

The main disagreements are about how far to go, not which levers matter. At the pause end, MIRI endorses the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act of 2026, which would pause training when precursor capabilities appear ([MIRI](https://intelligence.org/2026/09/23/miris-position-on-the-ban-artificial-superintelligence-act-of-2026/)). MIRI's treaty proposal would cap training runs at 10^24 FLOP ([MIRI TGT](https://techgov.intelligence.org/research/an-international-agreement-to-prevent-the-premature-creation-of-artificial-superintelligence)). IAPS prefers a domestic "pacing framework" of industry agreements on limits ([IAPS](https://www.iaps.ai/research/priorities-for-frontier-ai-policy)). On liability, Gabriel Weil argues for strict liability and punitive damages calibrated to near-misses ([SSRN](https://ssrn.com/abstract=4694006)). The Institute for Law & AI stresses liability's limits for uninsurable catastrophes ([LawAI](https://law-ai.org/the-limits-of-liability/)).

Compute thresholds are also decaying as proxies. Training at 10^26 FLOP is projected to cost about $1M by 2028 ([arXiv 2408.06210](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.06210)), so anchors should credit capability triggers and thresholds a regulator can adjust.

Three conclusions follow for the rubric. First, **binding frontier law exists only in the EU, California, New York, Illinois and Korea**. Korea's AI Basic Act sets a 10^26 FLOP "high-performance AI" tier, in force since 22 January 2026 but with fines deferred for at least a year ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)). Second, **weight security, halt authority and strict liability have no enacted example anywhere**. Under fixed anchors the top level of those elements will be empty, which accurately reports that no state meets the standard. Third, oversight of internal deployment is where current thinking has moved, so a rubric built only on the 2023–2024 literature would already be out of date.

## Three-quarters of frontier compute sits in one country

The distribution of frontier activity makes one universal scale inappropriate. Epoch AI's data put about **75% of tracked GPU-cluster performance in the United States and about 15% in China**, with Germany, Japan and France playing "marginal roles" ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-supercomputers-performance-share-by-country)). The home countries of frontier developers, taken from the Seoul commitments and EU Code signatory lists, are the US, China, France, Canada, the UAE, South Korea, Germany and Italy ([Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366585914/AI-Seoul-Summit-16-AI-firms-make-voluntary-safety-commitments); [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/contents-code-gpai)). The binding frontier statutes, scoped at 10^25 to 10^26 FLOP and $500M in revenue, reach only a handful of developers ([METR](https://metr.org/notes/2026-01-29-frontier-ai-safety-regulations/)). Scoring Malawi or Uruguay low for lacking a frontier-developer statute would record "not applicable" as "bad governance". It would also turn the map into a picture of who hosts AI labs, which mostly means the Global North.

The **NTI Nuclear Security Index** is the closest methodological precedent. It runs separate rankings with different indicator sets:

- **Secure Materials:** the 22 countries holding 1 kg or more of weapons-usable material.
- **Support Global Efforts:** the 154 countries and areas holding less, scored only on global norms, domestic commitments and capacity, and risk environment.
- **Protect Facilities:** a sabotage ranking for the 48 countries with nuclear facilities.

([NTI Index](https://www.ntiindex.org/about-the-nti-index/)). The Climate Action Tracker similarly applies climate-finance and net-zero elements only "where applicable" ([CAT](https://climateactiontracker.org/countries/rating-system/)). The GHSI is the counterexample: it scored every country on the same capacity framework, and its validity problems are well documented ([PMC10910940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10910940/)).

The x-risk literature implies which levers belong to which track. Developer obligations and pre-deployment evaluation apply where frontier developers sit. Compute levers (licensing, KYC for cloud and chip buyers, anti-diversion enforcement) apply where large compute is hosted or the chip supply chain runs through. The Gulf build-out, backed by US-approved chip exports to G42 and Humain, puts the UAE and Saudi Arabia in this middle tier ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/11/20/us-approves-ai-chip-exports-to-gulf-after-saudi-crown-prince-visit.html)). For everyone else the literature implies a smaller set of expectations. A country should join and implement frontier-specific international commitments and evaluation networks, cooperate against chip diversion, and keep channels for incidents and threat intelligence ([IAPS](https://www.iaps.ai/research/priorities-for-frontier-ai-policy); [GovAI](https://www.governance.ai/research-paper/computing-power-and-the-governance-of-artificial-intelligence)). No source proposes a verified "minimum package" for non-frontier states. That tier is an inference from the IAPS, GovAI and MIRI proposals.

Two further lessons from NTI. Track membership should rest on a **published, observable threshold**, as NTI's 1 kg rule does, and not on the LLM's judgment. And "not applicable" must render as its own state, distinct from 1. Continuous relevance weights, such as scaling a score by compute share, would mix exposure with quality and make scores hard to explain. Both NTI and CAT avoid them.

## LLM coders follow label names, not definitions, unless anchors name artefacts

The pipeline's LLM coding is sound for simple, factual items and fragile for evaluative constructs. On relevance, stance and frame tasks, ChatGPT beat crowd workers by about 25 percentage points ([Gilardi et al., PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2305016120)). Country-level governance judgments are harder. Coding V-Dem democracy indicators, "some LLMs are too pessimistic, while others consistently overestimate the democratic quality". The direction of the bias is "not known a priori", and combining models reduces it ([Weidmann et al., arXiv 2503.22315](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.22315)). A 2026 political event-coding study found that when label definitions were remapped, models kept the original labels 51.5–57.7% of the time, and reordering the codebook changed about 20% of predictions. The authors conclude that "high accuracy does not necessarily make an LLM a faithful coder" ([He et al., arXiv 2606.06781](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06781)). Outputs also vary across semantically equivalent prompts, least on knowledge-anchored tasks ([Liu, arXiv 2604.16413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16413)). LLM factual accuracy favours Global North statements over Global South ones ([Global-Liar, arXiv 2401.17839](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17839)).

For a frontier-risk dimension this has four design consequences.

**Write anchors as checkable artefacts, not adjectives.** "A statute in force requires pre-deployment evaluation of models above a stated threshold, and the regulator has reported at least one evaluation" is a fact the model can find and cite. "Strong frontier oversight" invites the model to fall back on what it already associates with the phrase. The app's `rationale` field already requires the single fact behind each score. For this dimension, a score above 2 should require a named instrument, body or agreement.

**Keep "insufficient evidence" apart from 1.** The current "when torn, give the lower" rule interacts with the geographic skew in sources: a data-poor country slides to the floor. Frontier-risk facts are sparse, often in national languages, and politically sensitive (China especially). The dimension should allow abstention, rendered as low confidence rather than a score.

**Test adherence, not only accuracy.** The ten-country gold set measures agreement with reference scores. The literature recommends further behavioural checks: shuffle anchor order, paraphrase prompts, and score hypothetical country vignettes with known answers, as V-Dem does ([V-Dem](https://www.v-dem.net/about/v-dem-project/methodology/)). The drift check should also report signed mean error, which shows whether a model is systematically optimistic or pessimistic. Mean absolute error hides that direction. In practice, all ten current gold scores are still unverified drafts, and a new normative dimension should not launch on unverified gold scores.

**Move deterministic facts out of the LLM.** Summit signatories, network members and CoE Convention signatures are public lists. The Bletchley Declaration has 29 signatories plus New Zealand. The measurement network has ten members: Australia, Canada, the EU, France, Japan, Kenya, Korea, Singapore, the UK and the US ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-safety-summit-2023-the-bletchley-declaration/the-bletchley-declaration-by-countries-attending-the-ai-safety-summit-1-2-november-2023); [Australia DISR](https://industry.gov.au/news/australia-meets-global-partners-ai-evaluation-and-measurement)). Coding these once into a committed reference file, as the app already does with `blocs.json`, removes a class of hallucination. It also makes the universal track cheap for the roughly 180 countries where little else applies.

## A frontier-risk score will read as advocacy unless it declares its standard

Framing is the biggest threat to the credibility of this dimension. The "AI ethics versus AI safety" divide is well documented. The literature describes it as "present-day harms" against "long-term risks", and critics charge that the safety field's ties to effective altruism and longtermism "foster ideological homogeneity" ([arXiv 2603.14495](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.14495)). Gebru and Torres's TESCREAL paper ties the AGI project to a bundle of ideologies that includes effective altruism; its reception is contested ([First Monday](https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/view/13636)). AI Now argues that AGI and race narratives "obscure structural harms and market concentration" ([AI Now 2025](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/research/ai-now-2025-landscape-report)). Journalism on Open Philanthropy's role in seeding congressional AI fellowships put EA funding networks under scrutiny ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/10/17/ai-regulation-influenced-open-philanthropy-fellows)). In US politics a pro-industry super PAC with more than $125M has attacked candidates as aligned with "effective altruism" ([Wikipedia: Leading the Future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_the_Future)). A score labelled "x-risk" from a project that states no mission will be read through that lens.

"More regulation is safer" is also contested from inside the technical community. Andrew Ng calls the claim that closed models are safer "regulatory capture" ([Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/tech/26/07/60716258/andrew-ng-backs-jensen-huangs-open-ai-push-says-closed-models-are-safer-pr-is-regulatory-capture)). Hinton, Fei-Fei Li and Ng all made a case for keeping AI open ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/12/as-ai-safety-concerns-mount-three-pioneers-make-the-case-for-staying-open/)). Seán Ó hÉigeartaigh, himself from the x-risk community, argues that the US–China "AI race" narrative has been used to justify less oversight and may become self-fulfilling ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5278644)). The IASR notes that released open weights "cannot be recalled" but does not prescribe a ban ([IASR 2026](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-extended-summary-policymakers)). The defensible position is to score whether a state can **see, test and stop** a dangerous model. Openness itself is not the target, and the pause camp's priority fits inside the rubric as one element, halt authority, not as the only route to a high score.

Three sources of bias need explicit handling:

- **Export controls.** Controls that the US and its allies score as strong governance are, for China and much of the Global South, constraints imposed from outside. A compute indicator should measure a state's visibility and control over compute in its own jurisdiction, not whether it aligns with US controls.
- **The compliance template.** Scoring "published a frontier safety framework" or "signed the Seoul or EU voluntary commitments" builds in a Western model of voluntary company commitments. FLI's own 2026 index added a "Chinese Regulatory Context" section to account for binding state rules shaping Chinese firms differently ([FLI](https://futureoflife.org/ai-safety-index-summer-2026/)). On China the evidence genuinely splits. Concordia documents AI risk in the national emergency plan and a surge of standards ([Concordia 2025](https://concordia-ai.com/research/state-of-ai-safety-in-china-2025/)). Other analysts find that the Chinese safety association CnAISDA "lacks dedicated staff, a budget of its own" and that "frontier AI safety is a secondary priority" ([AI Frontiers](https://ai-frontiers.org/articles/is-china-serious-about-ai-safety)). Separate sub-indicators for commitment, capacity and implementation let both camps see where they disagree.
- **Names.** The US and UK "safety to security" renames were criticised as a pivot away from long-term risk ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/from-safety-to-security-renaming-the-us-ai-safety-institute-is-not-just-semantics/)). Yet CAISI still tests unreleased models for cyber, bio and chemical risk, the core catastrophic categories ([NIST CAISI](https://www.nist.gov/caisi)). Score the function, not the label.

Credible raters do not claim neutrality. They publish the standard they rate against. Freedom House names its human-rights basis. CAT names the 1.5°C benchmark and keeps its equity judgment in a separate rating. WJP weights factors equally to accommodate different definitions of rule of law ([WJP](https://worldjusticeproject.org/news/understanding-wjp-rule-law-index-faq)). Two frontier-rating practices serve as warnings. FLI's panel weights indicators at each panelist's discretion and keeps individual grades confidential, so its grades cannot be reproduced ([FLI](https://futureoflife.org/ai-safety-index-summer-2026/)). And four of nine companies did not answer FLI's 2026 survey, leaving their grades to rest on public material alone; this is reported by an aggregator ([Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/fli-ai-safety-index-2026-enterprise-buyer-readout)). The app is already stronger than either on reproducibility: it publishes its rubric, prompt version, calibration breaks and evidence records, and it has a public "Report an issue" channel. That channel is the natural right of reply for governments, which rarely answer surveys.

## Recommended design: one benchmark-referenced dimension, four sub-indicators, three tracks

### Add a named lens and leave maturity as it is

Add a seventh scored dimension, `frontier_risk` ("Frontier Risk Governance"). It is normative, but **excluded from the `avg_score` maturity composite**, in the same way `governance_type` and `actor_involvement` are excluded today. Three reasons support keeping it out. First, maturity answers "how much AI governance is in force", while this dimension answers "how close is the state to a stated standard for catastrophic-risk governance", and averaging the two recreates the readiness–safety conflation the OECD critiques. Second, excluding it preserves the maturity series that rubric v3 made comparable over time. Third, an x-risk lens sitting beside the other six, rather than overwriting them, is the synthesis the ethics–safety literature supports ([Gyevnar & Kasirzadeh, arXiv 2502.09288](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09288)). Kasirzadeh's "accumulative x-risk" account, in which social harms compound into existential ones, supports cross-linking the two ([Philosophical Studies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02301-3)). It does not support merging them.

For the maintainer's "5 means implemented, not good" concern, add a sentence to `methodology.html` stating that the maturity dimensions measure the extent of implementation and are not quality ratings. Quality against a catastrophic-risk standard lives only in the new dimension. Do not retrofit quality judgments into the v3 anchors. That would force a calibration break and mix the two constructs.

### Assign tracks from data the LLM never touches

Assign each country one of three tracks in a committed file (for example `public/data/frontier_tracks.json`), with the evidence behind each assignment:

- **Track H (frontier host)** is a country that is home to a developer of a model at or above 10^25 FLOP, the EU presumption threshold, per Epoch AI's model database, which the OECD.AI Index already uses ([OECD.AI Index paper](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2026/02/oecd-ai-observatory-index_8f5fa0f2/32c01014-en.pdf)). The provisional list is the US, China, France, Canada, South Korea, Germany, the UAE and the UK, to be confirmed against Epoch data before launch.
- **Track C (compute and chokepoint)** is a country hosting frontier-scale clusters in Epoch's GPU-cluster data, or a node in the chip supply chain. Candidates include Saudi Arabia, Japan, Malaysia, Singapore, the Netherlands and Taiwan. This list was not researched and needs its own verification pass ([Epoch GPU clusters](https://epoch.ai/data/gpu-clusters)).
- **Track G (global)** covers every other country.

EU member states should **inherit the EU-level developer-obligation score with a visible "EU-level" flag**. National credit for one Brussels regulation is exactly the transposition effect GIRAI documents.

### Aggregate non-compensatorily and show the track

The dimension score is the mean of the applicable sub-indicators (four for H, three for C, two for G). It is capped at the lowest applicable sub-indicator plus 1, which puts the OECD/JRC Handbook's warning about compensation into practice. Under that cap, the UK's likely 5 on international coordination cannot lift it past 3 while its developer obligations sit at 2. The capped score says what the literature says: the UK is a world-class evaluator without a binding frontier regime. Show the track in the tooltip and legend. Render NA and insufficient evidence apart from 1, using the existing hatch or a neutral fill. Allow comparison across tracks only on the two universal sub-indicators, as NTI publishes separate rankings.

Before launch, run a JRC-style sensitivity check comparing arithmetic, geometric and capped aggregation on the gold countries, and publish how much ranks move ([JRC audit of GII](https://www.wipo.int/web-publications/global-innovation-index-2025/en/appendix-ii-joint-research-centre-jrc-statistical-audit-of-the-2025-global-innovation-index.html)). For federations, the maintainer must choose which layer counts. For the US, the choice is between the federal layer, with California and New York noted in the rationale, and the strongest layer covering most domestic frontier developers. The federal executive is litigating against state AI laws ([Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/client-alerts/president-trump-signs-executive-order-challenging-state-ai-laws)), so this choice moves the US score by up to two points and should be published.

### Anchor to published frameworks and publish a value statement

Name the reference standard on the methodology page and cite each sub-indicator to it:

- **Risk taxonomy:** the International AI Safety Report 2026 (malicious use, malfunctions including loss of control, systemic risks) and the EU Code of Practice's four systemic risks: CBRN, loss of control, cyber offence and harmful manipulation. CSET calls the Code "a minimum standard for appropriate risk management for frontier AI models" ([CSET](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/eu-ai-code-safety/)).
- **Obligation checklist:** METR's frontier-regulation reference fields.
- **Binding-versus-voluntary coding:** the accountability chain in arXiv 2608.19278.
- **Leading edge of the standard:** the IAPS 2026 priorities (internal deployment, weight security).
- **Structure and aggregation:** the NTI Index for tracks, and the OECD/JRC Handbook.
- **Comparability:** the OECD.AI Index's planned mandatory-testing and incident-manual items.

Publish a short value statement: this lens rates governance of catastrophic risk from frontier AI against the standard above. It is normative, it does not rate rights protection, and it sits beside the descriptive layers, which it leaves unchanged. The statement should cite the critics (AI Now, the ethics–safety divide literature) alongside the EA-aligned sources, and disclose funding and who designed the rubric.

### Pipeline changes

The new dimension does not change existing anchors, so `RUBRIC_VERSION` stays; `PROMPT_VERSION` bumps and the new series starts without a calibration break. Four further changes are recommended:

- **Research depth by track.** Run full web-search research on `frontier_risk` only for the ~15 Track H and C countries. For Track G, derive sub-indicator 4 from the committed lists and sub-indicator 3 from one targeted search per country. The existing run already uses ~140k input tokens per country, so this keeps cost close to today's.
- **Evidence floor.** Require a cited artefact for any score above 2, and emit `insufficient_evidence` rather than defaulting to the lower level.
- **Gold set before launch.** Add frontier-risk gold scores for about eight countries across all three tracks, hand-verified by the maintainer, and report signed bias in `drift.json`.
- **Cross-validation.** Report correlations with the OECD AISI flag, GIRAI Trust & Safety and Oxford Insights Resilience. The correlations should be low; a high one would mean the dimension is re-measuring readiness.

## Conclusion

A frontier-risk dimension built this way should have a sparse top and a large NA region, and both are its main value. Seen through the literature on catastrophic risk, even the leading jurisdictions sit at 3–4. The UK leads on evaluation but has no binding frontier law. The EU has binding law but no documented enforcement. The US has voluntary testing, and its federal government is litigating against the states that legislated. China has emergency plans but institutions of contested substance. No state has weight-security mandates, halt authority or oversight of internal deployment. The current maturity scores cannot express any of this, because they reward implementing whatever exists. A lens that uses published frontier-safety frameworks as its benchmark can express it, provided each anchor names a checkable artefact and one weak element caps the whole score.

The larger open question is validity, and it deserves stating plainly on the methodology page. No study links any of these governance indicators to lower catastrophic risk, and the GHSI's inverse correlation with COVID outcomes shows how far scores of capacity on paper can mislead. The dimension should therefore be presented as a transparent audit of whether a state can see, test and stop a dangerous model, against a declared standard. It should not claim to measure how safe a country is. If the app offers a public right of reply, publishes its gold set and signed bias, and treats "no evidence" as different from "no activity", it would be more reproducible than any existing frontier scorecard. That credibility is also the best defence against the charge of advocacy.
